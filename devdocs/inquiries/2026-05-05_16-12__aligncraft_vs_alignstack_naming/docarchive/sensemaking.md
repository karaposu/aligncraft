# Sensemaking — AlignCraft vs AlignCraft Naming

## User Input
`devdocs/inquiries/2026-05-05_16-12__aligncraft_vs_AlignCraft_naming/_branch.md`

Question: Is "AlignCraft" a better book title than the current "AlignCraft" given what the book preaches?
Goal: A clear verdict + reasoning that ties each name's connotations to the book's thesis.
Exploration produced a pivot question: "Should the book name reflect its STRUCTURE (a stack of layers) or its SUBSTANCE (a practiced discipline)?"

---

## SV1 — Baseline Understanding

The user is asking which of two names — AlignCraft (current) or AlignCraft (proposed) — better fits a book teaching alignment-based AI-assisted development. The user signals low attachment to the current name and explicitly says brand-transition cost is irrelevant. The decision should be made on fit-to-content alone.

At first read, both names share the load-bearing prefix "Align" and differ only in suffix: "Stack" (architectural, layered) vs "Craft" (practiced, artisan). Both seem defensible. Without analysis, the question feels like personal taste.

---

## Phase 1 — Cognitive Anchor Extraction

### Constraints
- **C1.** The "Align" prefix is fixed — alignment is the book's thesis word; replacing it would break the book.
- **C2.** The name must work as a community brand (LinkedIn group, future events, GitHub repo).
- **C3.** The book is already published with a name; switching has documentation churn even if user has waived sunk-cost concern.
- **C4.** The decision is fit-to-content, not market-data-driven (no A/B test data exists).
- **C5.** The name must be a single coined word (both candidates obey this; the comparable form is the fair test).

### Key Insights
- **I1.** The book has a structural duality: a *taxonomy* (six layers) and a *practice* (four pillars + patterns + workflows). The taxonomy is stack-shaped; the practice is craft-shaped.
- **I2.** The book's own positioning statement — "codifies what developers do instinctively, turning scattered practices into a cohesive methodology" — is a substance claim, not a structure claim. It describes what craft is.
- **I3.** The opening sentence "AlignCraft is a stack of six fundamental alignment layers" is *internally tautological with the current name*. The name itself is doing definitional work, which means the name shapes how the book frames itself, not just how it markets itself.
- **I4.** "Stack" is overloaded in the dev community by tech-stack ("what's your stack?"). When developers hear "AlignCraft," priming pulls them toward "set of layered tools" — i.e., an inventory.
- **I5.** "Craft" carries the Software Craftsmanship lineage (Pete McBreen 2002, Manifesto for Software Craftsmanship 2009). Anyone in the senior-IC/quality-conscious audience hears that lineage.
- **I6.** "Stack" reads as a static noun (you *have* a stack); "Craft" reads as both noun and verb (you have a craft and you craft things). This matters for a methodology book whose central act is *practice*.

### Structural Points
- **S1.** The book is composed of: framing (the six layers as a diagnostic taxonomy) + body (patterns and practices) + tools (commands/skills/hooks) + appendices (prompts).
- **S2.** Page-count and substance weight tilt heavily toward body+tools (the practice), not framing (the taxonomy).
- **S3.** The taxonomy serves the practice: layers exist so practitioners can *know where to look* when alignment fails — diagnostic, not architectural.

### Foundational Principles
- **P1.** A book name is a *promise to the reader* about what they'll get. A name signals genre: methodology, reference, manifesto, craft manual, etc.
- **P2.** A name that contradicts the book's center of gravity creates dissonance every time someone reads the title and then reads the body.
- **P3.** Coined words inherit connotations from morpheme parts; you cannot opt out of those connotations by fiat.

### Meaning-Nodes
- **M1. Alignment** — central thesis word, locked.
- **M2. Stack** — layered architecture, technical infrastructure, "what you have."
- **M3. Craft** — practiced discipline, artisan tradition, "what you do."
- **M4. Methodology** — the book's self-described category.
- **M5. Codification of instinct** — the book's stated mission, which is the *literal definition of craft*.

#### SV2 — Anchor-Informed Understanding

The question is no longer about taste. It's about whether the name should foreground the book's diagnostic framing (six-layer taxonomy → "Stack") or its operational mission (codifying instinct into practiced discipline → "Craft"). Anchor I2 is decisive-looking: the book's own positioning describes what craft is. But this might be premature, so it needs perspective testing.

## Phase 2 — Perspective Checking

### Technical / Logical
- A name's job is to be a stable referent that primes correct interpretation. From a pure information-theoretic standpoint, "AlignCraft" reduces priming entropy when paired with "six layers" (the title pre-explains the structure). "AlignCraft" requires more setup because "craft" doesn't pre-announce six layers.
- New anchor: **N1.** Stack is a *self-explanatory framing device for the taxonomy*. Craft is not.

### Human / User (the reader)
- A senior developer who picked up this book wants to know: *will this make me better at working with AI?* — not *will this give me a six-layer taxonomy?*
- "Craft" signals "this is for people who care about the practice." "Stack" signals "this is a system." The book is more the former than the latter.
- New anchor: **N2.** Craft self-selects the right audience (senior ICs who already value methodology); Stack risks attracting and disappointing readers expecting a tech-stack guide.

### Strategic / Long-term
- A community brand for a methodology grows by people *practicing* it and *teaching it*. "AlignCraft community," "AlignCraft practitioners," "let me show you my AlignCraft" — these phrases land as practice.
- "AlignCraft community," "AlignCraft users" — these phrases land as users-of-a-system.
- New anchor: **N3.** Craft enables practitioner-language; Stack enables user-language. For a methodology that wants discipleship, practitioner-language is structurally stronger.

### Risk / Failure
- **Stack-risks:** mistaken for a tech-stack guide (failure to communicate genre); reads as static catalog (mismatches body content); ages with dev-jargon evolution.
- **Craft-risks:** gaming-suffix association ("Minecraft, AlignCraft" — could be twee); "craft" can read precious in some corporate contexts; might feel romantic when the content is rigorous.
- The Stack-risks are about *miscommunication of the book's genre*. The Craft-risks are about *register/tone*. Miscommunication of genre is a stronger failure than tonal preciousness.
- New anchor: **N4.** Craft's downside is tonal; Stack's downside is genre-confusion. Genre-confusion is the worse failure.

### Resource / Feasibility
- User has explicitly waived sunk-cost concerns. Renaming is feasible: README files, community names, command pack references, logo. None are blocking.
- New anchor: **N5.** Feasibility neutral — does not influence the decision.

### Definitional / Consistency
- Does "AlignCraft" contradict its own definition? Look at the opening: "AlignCraft is a stack of six fundamental alignment layers for AI-driven action." The name is *internally consistent with the taxonomy section* but contradicts the body's mission ("codifies what developers do instinctively").
- This is a self-contradicting definition: the name promises a stack of layers; the mission promises a codified practice. The name's purpose (stack of layers) is real; the mission's purpose (practice) is also real. They don't fit under one metaphor.
- AlignCraft would force the opening sentence to change — "AlignCraft is the practice of aligning across six layers of AI-driven action" — and that revised sentence is *more consistent with the rest of the book* than the current opening is.
- New anchor: **N6.** The current name produces an internal inconsistency between framing and mission. Switching to AlignCraft resolves it; the opening becomes a unified statement of practice across layers, rather than an inventory of layers.

### Adversarial perspective (the uncomfortable one — most likely to break the model)
- "What if the *taxonomy* is the book's actual contribution and the practice content is just commentary on the taxonomy?" — i.e., the six layers ARE the original contribution; the rest is well-trodden methodology framing.
- If true, then "AlignCraft" is correct because the book's IP is the layers themselves, and the layers are stack-shaped.
- Counter-test: how much of the book is original taxonomy vs. how much is original practice? Reading the chapters list (Archaeology, Offload, Vibe Testing, DevDocs, Anchor, Probe Tests, Fuzzy Architecture, Slash Commands, Devdocs Folder Convention, etc.), the *original IP* is heavily on the practice side. The taxonomy is a useful framing device, but the original work is in the patterns.
- New anchor: **N7.** Even from the adversarial perspective, the book's original contribution is on the practice side. The taxonomy is a frame, not the IP.

#### SV3 — Multi-Perspective Understanding

Across all six perspectives, the same pattern emerges from different angles: the book's center of gravity, original IP, audience-fit, community-fit, and self-consistency all favor a practice metaphor over a structural metaphor. Two anchors push the other way (N1: Stack pre-explains the taxonomy; N7's setup: the layers are the IP) but both fail their own tests on closer inspection. The Stack-side argument is real but smaller than it first appears.

## Phase 3 — Ambiguity Collapse

### Ambiguity 1: What is the book's primary contribution — taxonomy or practice?

**Strongest counter-interpretation:** The six-layer alignment taxonomy is the original intellectual contribution; the practices (DevDocs, Anchor, Probe, etc.) are well-known patterns repackaged under the taxonomy. The taxonomy is what makes the book novel; therefore the name should foreground the taxonomy.

**Why the counter-interpretation fails (structural grounds):** The taxonomy is a single page of content (six layers + four pillars). The patterns occupy 12+ chapters. If the taxonomy were the contribution, the book would be a paper, not a 250+ page methodology with appendices. The structural ratio of content to taxonomy is roughly 50:1 in favor of practice. Additionally, several patterns (Archaeology, Devdocs Convention, Fuzzy Architecture) appear novel in this synthesis, not just repackaging. The taxonomy serves the patterns by giving readers a diagnostic frame; the patterns are the load-bearing content.

**Confidence:** HIGH — the page-count ratio and the originality of the patterns are concrete structural evidence, not just precedent.

**Resolution:** The book's primary contribution is the **practiced methodology** (patterns + workflows + tools). The taxonomy is a diagnostic frame *for* the practice.

**What is now fixed?** The center of gravity of the book is established as the practice content.

**What is no longer allowed?** Treating the taxonomy as the primary IP. Naming choices that foreground "stack" as if the layers were the product.

**What now depends on this choice?** The verdict on the name. Sensemaking can now legitimately weight craft-metaphor higher than stack-metaphor on fit-to-content grounds.

**What changed in the conceptual model?** The book is reclassified from "six-layer alignment framework" to "practiced methodology organized around six-layer diagnostic frame." The frame supports the practice; the practice is the substance.

---

### Ambiguity 2: Does "Craft" carry too much tonal baggage (gaming, twee artisanship) to function as a serious methodology brand?

**Strongest counter-interpretation:** "Craft" in 2026 is contaminated by Minecraft/StarCraft (gaming) and by artisan-coffee-shop preciousness. A serious methodology book aiming at senior engineers should avoid it.

**Why the counter-interpretation fails (structural grounds):** The Software Craftsmanship movement (McBreen 2002, Manifesto 2009, multiple ongoing professional conferences and books) has been the dominant framing for senior-engineer professional identity for two decades. "Clean Coder" by Robert Martin, the entire Software Craftsmanship lineage, "The Pragmatic Programmer" subtitle "From Journeyman to Master" — all use craft framing successfully in the exact target audience. The gaming association exists but is recessive in professional dev contexts (compare: nobody confuses "software craftsmanship" with Minecraft). Tonal preciousness is a real risk only if the body doesn't support the framing — and this book's body explicitly supports it.

**Confidence:** HIGH — the empirical track record of Software Craftsmanship as a serious senior-developer brand is direct counter-evidence.

**Resolution:** "Craft" is a viable serious-methodology suffix in the target audience.

**What is now fixed?** Tonal-baggage objection is downgraded from blocker to background concern.

**What is no longer allowed?** Dismissing AlignCraft on tonal grounds without engaging the Software Craftsmanship lineage.

**What now depends on this choice?** The Craft-risks identified in N4 are now bounded — they don't break the candidate.

**What changed in the conceptual model?** Craft is no longer "playful but risky"; it's "serious in the right audience, with a recessive playful association that aids memorability."

---

### Ambiguity 3: Does the existing brand investment (LinkedIn community, deployed website, prior content) create a cost the user is underestimating?

**Strongest counter-interpretation:** Users routinely under-weight transition costs. A LinkedIn community of N members already knows the name; renaming creates discoverability friction; existing search results, links, and references all decay.

**Why the counter-interpretation fails (structural grounds):** This counter-argument is correct in general but inapplicable here because the user has *explicitly waived* this concern. Sensemaking cannot override stated user constraints absent contradicting evidence. Furthermore, the user's question is specifically "fit to what the book preaches" — they have factored switching cost out of the question deliberately. Re-injecting it would be answering a question they did not ask.

**Confidence:** HIGH on the bounded scope, LOW on whether the user is right to waive the cost — but bounded scope is what was asked.

**Resolution:** Sunk-cost is excluded from the verdict per user direction. Mentioned as a flag, not a factor.

**What is now fixed?** The verdict is rendered on fit-to-content alone.

**What is no longer allowed?** Adding "but switching is hard" as a tiebreaker.

**What now depends on this choice?** The downstream critique can focus on content-fit dimensions only.

**What changed in the conceptual model?** Decision space narrows: only fit-to-content matters.

#### SV4 — Clarified Understanding

The question reduces to a clean comparison on fit-to-content. The book's primary contribution is established as practice; "craft" is established as a viable serious-methodology suffix; sunk-cost is bounded out. The remaining variable is: does the substance-fit advantage of "Craft" beat the structure-fit advantage of "Stack" by enough to justify a switch?

## Phase 4 — Degrees-of-Freedom Reduction

### What variables are now fixed
- The "Align" prefix.
- The book's center of gravity is the practice (chapters + patterns + workflows + tools), not the taxonomy.
- The decision criterion is fit-to-content; sunk-cost excluded.
- "Craft" carries the Software Craftsmanship lineage as a credible senior-developer signal; gaming-suffix is recessive.
- "Stack" carries the tech-stack lineage as a likely-misleading signal in the dev community.

### What options are eliminated
- Defending AlignCraft on the basis that "the book has a stack of six layers" — the layers are a frame, not the substance.
- Rejecting AlignCraft on tonal grounds without engaging the Software Craftsmanship tradition.
- Invoking sunk-cost as a tiebreaker.

### What paths remain viable
- **Path A: Switch to AlignCraft** — name foregrounds practice; matches center of gravity; resolves the framing/mission inconsistency; carries credible craft lineage.
- **Path B: Keep AlignCraft** — name foregrounds the taxonomy; literally maps to "six layers"; preserves continuity; preserves a real-but-narrow framing benefit.
- **Path C (latent): Pick a third name** — e.g., AlignWorks. Less brandable than either; not requested by the user; deferred.

#### SV5 — Constrained Understanding

The decision is now between two well-characterized options on a single primary dimension: does the practice-fit advantage of AlignCraft outweigh the structure-fit advantage of AlignCraft? The substantive evidence (page-count ratio, original IP location, body-positioning statements, audience-priming, community-language) tilts toward AlignCraft. The case for AlignCraft rests almost entirely on its literal mapping to "six layers" — a benefit that appears once (in the opening sentence) and conflicts with the book's mission throughout.

## Phase 5 — Conceptual Stabilization

### SV6 — Stabilized Model

**The choice is between naming the frame and naming the work.**

The book contains both a frame (six alignment layers — diagnostic taxonomy) and the work (patterns, practices, workflows, tools that practitioners apply to maintain alignment across those layers). The current name *AlignCraft* names the frame. The proposed name *AlignCraft* names the work.

The book's center of gravity, original contribution, mission statement, target audience, and community ambitions all point at the work, not the frame. The frame exists to organize the work; the work is what readers learn and what practitioners do. A name should signal the book's *substance*, and the substance here is craft.

Three structural arguments converge:

1. **Substance fit** — the body of the book is overwhelmingly practice content. The taxonomy is one page; the practice is a book.
2. **Mission fit** — the book's stated mission ("codify what developers do instinctively") is the literal definition of craft.
3. **Internal consistency** — the current name forces an opening line ("AlignCraft is a stack of six layers") that is inconsistent with the body. AlignCraft would produce an opening ("AlignCraft is the practice of aligning across six layers of AI-driven action") that is consistent with the body.

The case for AlignCraft — that the title literally pre-explains the taxonomy — is a real but small benefit, paid for with a structural mismatch that recurs every time the book describes itself.

**Stabilized verdict (handed to critique for adversarial testing):** AlignCraft is a better fit. The verdict needs critique to stress-test it, but sensemaking has produced a clear preferred direction with explicit structural reasoning rather than vibes.

### Difference from SV1

SV1 read the question as a matter of taste. SV6 reads it as a structural decision with a clear answer: the book is *primarily* a craft, *organized around* a six-layer frame. The current name foregrounds the secondary aspect (the frame). The proposed name foregrounds the primary aspect (the practice). The structural evidence — page-count, IP location, mission statement, audience signals, internal consistency — converges on AlignCraft. The remaining work is for critique to attempt to break this verdict.
